The charging system

How the system works

  1. The crankshaft turns the alternator pulley through the drive belt, usually 2.5 to 3 times engine speed.
  2. The voltage regulator feeds a small current (2 to 6 A) through the brushes and slip rings into the rotor's field coil. That turns the spinning rotor into an electromagnet.
  3. The rotating magnetic field induces three-phase AC in the stationary stator windings.
  4. The rectifier's diodes turn that AC into DC at the B+ terminal. The output current leaves through the B+ cable, charges the battery and runs the loads, and comes back through the engine block and the alternator's case or ground terminal.
  5. The regulator watches system voltage. Below its setpoint (about 14.2 to 14.6 V for a 12 V system) it raises field current; above it, it cuts field current back. Output voltage is controlled by field current, not by engine speed.
  6. At key-on with the engine stopped, current flows through the charge lamp into the D+/exciter terminal to start the field. The lamp lights. Once the alternator is charging, the D+ terminal rises to system voltage, there's no voltage across the lamp, and it goes out.

Boat differences

  • On boats with gasoline engines, alternators in the engine compartment must be ignition-protected so brush sparks can't ignite gasoline vapor (USCG 33 CFR 183.410, ABYC E-11). Look for a marking such as SAE J1171, UL 1500, ISO 8846 or ABYC C-1500.
  • Boats often charge several battery banks through a battery switch, a diode isolator, or an automatic charging relay (ACR). A diode isolator drops about 0.6 to 0.8 V, so the regulator needs a battery sense wire or a higher setpoint to make up for it.
  • ABYC lets engine-mounted equipment return current through the engine block. Even so, engines on rubber mounts and alternators with isolated-ground designs need a dedicated ground cable to the alternator's negative terminal. Never rely on the mounting bracket alone.
  • Never turn the battery switch to OFF with the engine running. The load dump spike can destroy the rectifier diodes and regulator. A field disconnect on the switch, or an alternator protection device, prevents this.

Test reference (12 V system)

Rows marked ABYC E-11 use the E-11 voltage drop rule (E-11.15.1.2.6). E-11 sizes conductors for no more than 3% drop on panelboard main feeders, bilge blowers, electronics, navigation lights and other circuits where drop should be kept low, and 10% on general lighting and other non-critical circuits. The conductor length is measured from the positive source connection to the device and back to the negative source connection (E-11.15.1.2.7.3), so positive and negative drops add. E-11's worked example puts 3% at nominal 12 V at 0.36 V. E-11 does not name the alternator output circuit in either category. This trainer holds the charging circuit to the 3% limit as its own choice, measured at full alternator output. Rows marked Industry come from battery and automotive practice (BCI, SAE, manufacturers), not ABYC. ABYC publishes no alternator or charging-system test procedure.

ABYC requirements that touch the charging system

  • Voltage drop (E-11.15.1.2.6 and 11.15.1.2.7.3): conductors are sized for no more than 3% drop on panelboard main feeders, bilge blowers, electronics, navigation lights and other circuits where drop should be kept low, and 10% on general lighting and other non-critical circuits. Length is measured out and back, and E-11's example uses nominal system voltage (3% of 12 V is 0.36 V). The current is the circuit's load, or the panelboard's calculated load for main feeders. E-11 does not list the alternator output circuit in either group. A drop test under full current is how you check an installed circuit.
  • Overcurrent protection location (E-11.10.1.1.1.1): each ungrounded conductor needs a fuse or breaker within 7 inches of where it connects to the source of power, measured along the conductor. The exceptions that matter here:
    • Cranking motor conductors need no overcurrent protection.
    • A conductor connected directly to a battery terminal and run inside a sheath or enclosure for its whole length may have its protection up to 72 inches from the battery, placed as close as practicable.
    • A conductor connected somewhere other than a battery terminal and run inside a sheath or enclosure for its whole length may have its protection up to 40 inches from the connection, placed as close as practicable.
    • A self-limiting alternator with an integral regulator needs no protection on a conductor under 40 inches long that is sheathed for its whole length and does not connect to the battery.
    • No protection is needed at the output of a self-limiting device, alternators included, when the conductor's ampacity is at least the device's rated output.
    The alternator exceptions cover the alternator end only. Where the same conductor connects at a battery or bus, the battery is a source of power, so the 7-inch rule or the 72-inch sheathed exception applies there.
  • Battery switch (E-11.6.1.2): a switch is required in the positive conductor of each battery or bank over 800 CCA (or over 100 Ah when CCA isn't given). It goes as close to the battery as practicable, and its control must be readily accessible. Its 30-second rating must cover the largest cranking motor it serves, and the switch must meet ABYC C-7. Neither E-11 nor C-7 requires protecting the alternator when the switch is turned off. ABYC E-13 (lithium batteries) does require the system to protect components when a battery management system disconnects the battery (E-13.6.10.1). Its Appendix 2 notes that turning off a battery switch while an alternator is charging can also cause a voltage transient.
  • Ignition protection (E-11.5.1.4.1, 33 CFR 183.410): potential ignition sources in spaces with gasoline engines or gasoline fuel system fittings must be ignition-protected unless isolated from the fuel source. The federal rule applies to boats with gasoline engines other than outboards (33 CFR 183.401). It requires that a component not ignite a 4.25 to 5.25% propane and air mixture at its rated voltages and loads. E-11 lists ABYC C-1500, UL 1500, SAE J1171 and ISO 8846 as test standards (E-11.4.20).
  • Battery installation (E-10.7.4 and 10.7.8): an installed battery must not move more than 1 inch when pulled with twice its own weight for one minute each: vertically, fore and aft, and to each side. The ungrounded terminal and any bare cell straps must be protected from contact with metal objects. A nonconductive boot or shield, a covered nonconductive battery box, or a nonconductive compartment for the battery alone all meet this. E-11.5.2.8 also requires positive battery terminals, and both ends of the wires connected to them, to be covered (boots, for example).
  • DC negative and grounding (E-11.5.2): DC systems are two-wire, but engine-mounted equipment is exempt, so the alternator may return current through the block (E-11.5.2.1). The battery negative cable lands at the engine negative terminal (E-11.4.12). On boats with more than one engine with a grounded cranking motor, the engine negative terminals must be tied together by a conductor that can carry each engine's cranking motor current (E-11.5.2.4.1). E-11 gives no fixed size for a single engine's negative cable. Cranking motor conductors should meet the engine maker's sizing (note to E-11.15.1.2.7.2), and in practice the negative cable matches the positive cranking cable.
  • Charging indicator (P-4.7.1.4): inboard propulsion engines need an indicator at the main helm that shows when the charging system is not working.

Your diagnosis

Suggested test sequence
  1. Key off: read resting battery voltage. Clamp the battery cable for parasitic draw.
  2. Key off: battery load test.
  3. Key on, engine off: does the charge lamp light? Read D+ to ground.
  4. Start the engine, 2000 rpm, light loads: read voltage across the battery posts.
  5. Heavy loads: repeat. Then switch to the carbon pile (full output) and check the drops: ALT B+ to BAT+, plus BAT− to ALT case. The two added together must be 0.36 V or less (ABYC E-11, 3%). Use BAT− to block to see whether the strap is the problem.
  6. Switch to AC volts: ripple from ALT B+ to ALT case.
  7. Output test with the load tester while running. Inspect the belt.

Simulated readings are simplified for training. Real systems vary by make; always follow the manufacturer's procedure and specifications.
